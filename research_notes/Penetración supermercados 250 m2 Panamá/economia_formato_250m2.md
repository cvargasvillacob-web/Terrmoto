## 1. Benchmarks from comparable LatAm proximity, express and hard-discount formats (store size, SKUs, sales per store/m², ticket, growth, margins)

### Takeaway
Successful LatAm small formats cluster in two models. Hard discount runs 250–450 m², about 800–1,200 SKUs, heavy private label, 16–24% gross margin and 3–10% EBITDA (D1, Ara, Ísimo, Mass, Tiendas 3B). Convenience runs about 100 m², about 3,000 SKUs and a high-margin, high-frequency mix (OXXO, Tambo+). Derived sales productivity is about US$2,500–3,000 per m² of sales area per year for Mass, 3B and Ísimo, and about US$5,000–6,500 per m² for the mature Colombian discounters (D1, Ara) and OXXO. The best-documented unit economics are Tiendas 3B's: about 450 m², about US$300k capex per store, 26-month payback, about 740 transactions per store per day and a US$4.9 average ticket.

Derivation notes:
- **Mass transactions per day:** sales per store ÷ 365 ÷ US$3.7. The ticket is about 2019, so this is very rough.
- **Ara sales per m²** looks high for hard discount. It may reflect large urban stores or a sales-area definition narrower than 350 m², so treat it as an upper bound.

Conclusions from the table:
- **Productivity range.** A credible 250 m² proximity supermarket benchmark is **US$2,500–3,000 per m² per year** for the "young, fast-growing hard-discount" profile (Mass, 3B, Ísimo) and **US$5,000–6,500 per m² per year** for mature leaders (D1, Ara, OXXO). For a Panama store of 250 m² that brackets annual sales at **US$0.63–1.6M**, i.e. **US$1,700–4,400 per day**.
- **Ticket.** Hard-discount tickets in Mexico and Peru are about US$4–5. Panama's price level and the 16–17% "ruta" price premium (Section 4) suggest a higher ticket for a proximity supermarket with fresh food, plausibly **US$7–12**. That is an assumption: no Panama ticket data was found. At US$9, US$2,700 a day needs about 300 transactions a day, well below 3B's ~740.
- **Gross margin.** Hard discounters run 16–17% (3B). Mass sits below SP's 23.5% blend. Panama's Grupo Rey multi-format supermarkets run 23.6–25%. A 250 m² Panama proximity supermarket that prices between supermarkets and "chinitos" could plausibly target **24–27%** (assumption).
- **Scale need.** EBITDA margins are thin even for leaders (Ara 3.4–4.1%, 3B ~5.6% adjusted, D1 net ~1.9%). Small-box grocery only works with scale in purchasing and logistics (DCs) and very tight store opex.

## 2. Typical cost structure for a 200–300 m² store (capex per m², inventory, staffing, utilities, shrink, DC logistics)

### Takeaway
Benchmarks point to **US$500–1,000 per m² of sales area** for store capex (Mass, 3B) plus initial inventory, so about **US$200–330k all-in for 250 m² in Panama** (estimate). Labor and electricity are Panama's structural disadvantages. My illustrative P&L puts **four-wall break-even at about US$0.8–1.05M of annual sales** (≈US$3,100–4,200 per m² per year; ≈US$2,100–2,900 per day). A 3B-like payback of about 2–3 years needs about US$1.5M or more per store at mid-market rents.

### Inferences
**Capex for 250 m² in Panama (estimate):**
- Fit-out and equipment (refrigeration, cold room, HVAC, shelving, POS, signage, electrical): 250 m² × US$600–1,000 = **US$150–250k**. The upper end applies if the shell is "gris" (raw), if a full fresh offer is included (butchery, produce, cold rooms), or if HVAC must be installed. HVAC is mandatory in Panama's climate and is a larger line than in Lima or Bogotá.
- Initial inventory: at US$1.0M sales and about 75% COGS, about 2–3 weeks of stock is **US$30–45k**, partly supplier-financed.
- Pre-opening costs (2–3 months' rent deposit, training, launch marketing, permits): **US$15–30k**.
- **Total: about US$200–325k.** This is consistent with Mass (US$100–150k for a smaller, simpler box) and 3B (~US$300k for 450 m²).

**Staffing model (estimate).** Opening hours of about 7:00–22:00 every day (~105 store-hours a week), with 3–5 people on the floor (cashier/stocker multitasking plus fresh), need **7–10 FTE including a store manager**. Loaded cost per minimum-wage FTE in Panama Region 1 is about **US$10.0–10.6k a year** (Section 4), so payroll is **US$80–115k a year**.

**Electricity (estimate; consumption intensity is my assumption, not sourced).** A 250 m² store with open refrigeration and 24/7 HVAC in a tropical climate probably draws **8,000–14,000 kWh a month**. At a Panama commercial all-in rate of about US$0.25–0.26/kWh (Section 4) that is **US$2,000–3,600 a month (US$24–43k a year)**, about 2–4% of sales. That is roughly double the share in Peru or Mexico. Efficient doors-on cases, LED lighting and rooftop solar are material levers.

**Shrink and logistics.** Budget **1.2–2.0% shrink** (higher with a large fresh offer) and **2.5–4% of sales for DC-to-store logistics** if not already in COGS. A small Panama operator without its own DC will pay more because of direct-store delivery and weaker purchasing terms.

**Illustrative four-wall P&L for a 250 m² store in Panama.** These are my own scenario calculations under the following assumptions:
- Variable costs = 5% of sales: card and banking 1.5%, DC logistics 3%, other variable costs 0.5%.
- Rent is quoted per m² of gross leasable area of about 300–320 m², plus maintenance, plus 7% ITBMS (Casco listing shows "+ITBMS").
- Loaded FTE cost US$10.4k.

| Scenario | Sales/yr | Gross margin | FTE | Rent + maintenance (US$/m²/month) | kWh/month | Four-wall EBITDA | Break-even sales/yr | Payback on capex |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lean (secondary plaza, e.g. La Chorrera / David) | US$1.0M | 26% | 7 | 9 + 2 | 8,000 | US$47k (4.7%) | US$0.78M (≈US$2,100/day) | ~4.7 yrs on US$220k |
| Base (mid-market, e.g. Brisas del Golf / Arraiján street plaza) | US$1.0M | 25% | 9 | 12 + 2.5 | 10,000 | −US$11k (−1.1%) | US$1.05M (≈US$2,900/day) | n/a |
| Base at US$1.3M | US$1.3M | 25% | 9 | 12 + 2.5 | 10,000 | US$49k (3.8%) | US$1.05M | ~5.7 yrs on US$280k |

What the scenarios show:
- **Rent discipline.** Prime-plaza rents of US$25–35 per m² make the format non-viable unless sales are far above US$1.5M. Rent should stay at or below about **5% of sales**, which in practice means asking rents of **about US$8–15 per m² per month**.
- **Payback.** Matching 3B's 26-month payback in Panama needs roughly **US$1.6–1.8M a year (≈US$6,500–7,000 per m²)** or a gross margin of 27% or more. Both are ambitious, so a **3–5-year store payback** is a more credible planning assumption.
- **Labor.** Payroll is the single biggest lever: each FTE is about 1% of sales at US$1.0M. A self-checkout or automation layer, 3B-style task simplification and case-ready fresh product have outsized impact.

## 3. Catchment and trade-area rules of thumb (walking radius, households/population per store, store density, cannibalization)

### Takeaway
LatAm proximity formats work on a **5–10 minute walk (about 400–1,000 m)**, with mature density of about **1 hard-discount store per 13,200 inhabitants (Colombia)** and about **1 OXXO per 4,700 inhabitants in Mexico's densest state**. Applied to metro Panama (~1.93M people in Panamá, San Miguelito, Arraiján and La Chorrera), Colombia-level density implies room for about **150–190 proximity supermarkets across all operators**. One store needs roughly **6,000–10,000 residents (about 2,000–3,000 households) within a 500–800 m walk** (estimate).

### Cited Findings
- A study of metro Toluca used **1,000 m** as the optimal walking distance for an adult to reach a food outlet. On that basis OXXO covered about 62% of the urban area. — [UAEMEX](http://ri.uaemex.mx/bitstream/handle/20.500.11799/67109/04+El+formato+de+tiendas+de+conveniencia.pdf?sequence=1)
- OXXO density was about 1 store per 4,700 people in Nuevo León versus 1 per 14,000 in central Mexico. The data is a few years old. — [El Financiero](https://www.elfinanciero.com.mx/empresas/seis-factores-clave-que-explican-el-exito-de-oxxo/)
- On the Puebla–Tlaxcala road the average spacing between OXXOs was about 1,400 m (2,400 m maximum). — [Escenario Tlaxcala](https://escenariotlx.com/oxxos-en-tlaxcala-despojan-a-tiendas-locales/)
- In an area under 1 km² there was on average a second OXXO for each existing one. — [UNAM IIEC](https://ru.iiec.unam.mx/2939/1/Eje5-202-Venegas-Gonzalez.pdf)
- Colombia has about 1 hard-discount store per **13,200 inhabitants** (Forbes). D1, Ara and Ísimo hold a 75% penetration share. — [jrivasa / Forbes](https://jrivasa.com/2025/06/16/forbes-d1-y-ara-tienen-una-penetracion-del-75-del-mercado-en-colombia/)

### Inferences
**Metro Panama capacity (my calculation).** The four core districts total about 1.93M people. At Colombia's hard-discount density (1 per 13,200) that is about **146 stores**; at 1 per 10,000, about **193**; at OXXO Nuevo León density (1 per 4,700, a convenience benchmark), about **410**. These are totals for all proximity operators, not one chain. A new entrant capturing 25–35% of that white space suggests **40–70 stores in metro Panama** as a long-run ceiling before interior cities (David, Santiago, Chitré, Colón, Coclé).

**Households needed per store.** Assumptions: US$1.0–1.3M in annual sales, a "loyal" household spending about US$25 a week (US$1,300 a year) at the store, and the store capturing about 30–40% of catchment households as regulars. Under those assumptions a store needs about **770–1,000 loyal households**, so about **2,000–3,300 households (≈6,500–10,500 people at about 3.2 persons per household, an assumed size)** within the walk zone.
- In dense areas (San Miguelito, Panamá district corregimientos, Arraiján and La Chorrera urban cores) that population sits within **400–600 m**.
- In lower-density subdivisions of Panamá Oeste, Norte and Este it may require **800–1,000 m**, or car and moto access with parking, which favors strip-mall plazas.

**Spacing and cannibalization rule (estimate).** Keep own stores at least **~700–1,000 m apart** in medium-density areas and at least **~500 m** in very dense corridors. Colombian discounters tolerate 6–7 blocks (about 600–700 m) against competitors. Use walking isochrones that respect barriers such as the Interamericana, Corredor Norte and Sur, and rivers. Panama's road network fragments walk-sheds heavily, so straight-line radii overstate catchments.

### Gaps
- No published catchment or minimum-household rule from Mass, D1, Ara or OXXO.
- No Census 2023 persons-per-household by district was found; 3.2 is an assumption.
- No Panama Norte or Panama Este district populations; these areas fall within Panamá district corregimientos and need INEC tables.
- No study of proximity-store density in Panama, including counts of the roughly 10,500 metro minisúpers (Section 4).

### Inferences
**Rent bands for model inputs** (per m² of gross leasable area per month, before maintenance and 7% ITBMS):

| Location type | Asking rent (US$/m²/month) | Maintenance (US$/m²/month) |
|---|---|---|
| Interior towns (David, Santiago, Colón) and older barrios (Río Abajo) | 5–12 | 0–2 |
| Panamá Oeste street plazas (La Chorrera, Arraiján outside malls) | 7–21 | ~2.5 |
| San Miguelito and Tumba Muerto neighborhood plazas | 14–24 | n/a |
| Lifestyle plazas (Costa Verde, Dorado, Costa del Este) | 30–35 | n/a |
| Malls (Westland) | ~50 | ~6 |

Rent conclusions:
- **Model base:** US$12/m² + US$2.50 maintenance in Panamá Oeste, Norte and Este and San Miguelito plazas; US$8–10 in interior cities.
- **Size discount:** larger units (300–455 m²) ask less per m² than 80–150 m² units, which favors a 250 m² sales / ~320 m² GLA box.

**Labor.** The loaded cost of a Region 1 supermarket-rate employee at 48 hours is about **US$643 a month base**, or **US$10.0–10.6k a year** at 1.30–1.38×. Region 2 (interior districts outside the Region 1 list) is about **US$8.2–8.7k**.
- The CSS step-ups in March 2027 and 2029 add about 1 pp of payroll each. Model payroll inflation of about **+3–5% a year**.
- An operator with fewer than five branches may legally pay the lower general retail rate (B/.3.02 in Region 1), but the difference is small (about 2%).

**Electricity.** Use **US$0.25–0.27/kWh all-in** for a commercial BTD customer in 2026, with a sensitivity of +10–18%. Panama's tariff is at Mexico or Chile levels, but cooling loads are structurally higher. Energy efficiency (closed cases, inverter HVAC, LED) and rooftop solar should be in the base capex.

**Price positioning.** The roughly 17% basket premium of chinitos and minisúpers over supermarkets (my calculation: 353.31/302.79 = +16.7%; 344.25/294.25 = +17.0%) is the space a proximity supermarket can occupy. Matching supermarket prices at chinito-level convenience should win share; pricing at a premium would leave no wedge against the chinito.

## 5. Lessons learned: why proximity formats succeed or fail (density, assortment, private label, price vs. chinitos, logistics, digital, rent)

### Takeaway
The winners (D1, Ara, Mass, 3B) combine a tight assortment of about 800–1,200 SKUs, heavy private label (≥50% of sales), dense clusters around their own DCs, low-capex rented boxes and strict labor productivity, and they grow only as fast as cash and logistics allow. **Justo & Bueno is the cautionary tale, and it failed in Panama itself.** It reached about 1,000 stores in Colombia and about 100–130 in Panama on supplier credit and unpaid rents, then collapsed in 2022 when a promised capital injection never arrived. OXXO's pause in Chile and Peru shows that even world-class proximity operators struggle outside their home density and supply chain.

### Inferences
Lessons for a Panama 250 m² rollout. These are synthesis, not sourced claims.
1. **Fund the rollout with equity and committed debt, not supplier float or landlord arrears.** Justo & Bueno's Panama exit (2022) means landlords, suppliers and MITRADEL will remember a small-box discounter that left debts. Expect tougher lease guarantees and supplier terms, and plan for proof of solvency.
2. **Cluster before you spread.** Open in tight clusters of about 10–20 stores within roughly 30–40 minutes of one DC or cross-dock (for example, Panamá Oeste: Arraiján and La Chorrera, or San Miguelito plus Panamá Norte) to make daily fresh and frequent replenishment economic. OXXO's Chile and Peru pause shows the cost of sub-scale density.
3. **Assortment.** About 1,500–3,000 SKUs is a reasonable middle ground for a "proximity supermarket", against about 900 in hard discount and about 6,000 in Carulla Express. Strong fresh (produce, chicken, bread) is the traffic driver against chinitos. Private label should target more than 25–35% of sales by year 3, as margin defense in a high-cost market. These targets are estimates.
4. **Price against the chinito, not the hypermarket.** A roughly 17% basket gap leaves room to price at or near supermarket levels with convenience hours and still earn a 24–27% gross margin.
5. **Rent discipline.** Keep occupancy at or below about 5% of sales. Avoid mall and lifestyle-plaza rents of US$30–50 per m², and prefer ground-floor street plazas of US$8–15 per m² with parking and moto access.
6. **Labor and energy.** These are Panama's structural cost penalties versus Peru and Colombia: min-wage FTEs cost about US$10k or more, and electricity costs about US$0.26/kWh with 24/7 cooling. Design for 7–9 FTE, use self-checkout or assisted checkout, and put efficient refrigeration and solar in base capex.
7. **Digital.** Mass, 3B and D1 succeeded as pure brick-and-mortar. Delivery integration (PedidosYa, Uber Eats, which already list convenience stores in Panama) is an add-on for incremental sales, not the core thesis. — [Uber Eats Panamá listing](https://www.ubereats.com/pa/category/panam%C3%A1-panam%C3%A1/convenience)
